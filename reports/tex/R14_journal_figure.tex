\maketitle

\noindent R9 spends three figures on a single idea: every rule has a position in
the sector plane, a position in the monitored-attractor plane, and dissipation
moves it from the first to the second. Five condensed layouts follow, all from
the same data (\rt{obc0}, $254$ rules with both descriptors) and the same design
rules --- marker area grows with the number of rules stacked in a cell, colour is
the family, arrows are light and thicken with the number of rules making the same
move, and a rule that does not move gets no arrow. Only the layout differs.

\vspace{1em}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\ifgraphic{../../figures/fig_journal_overlay_obc0.pdf}{0.60\textwidth}
\caption{\textbf{A --- overlay.} One plane; each rule appears twice, hollow where
its sectors put it and filled where its attractors do, joined by an arrow. The
only layout in which both coordinates live on \emph{the same axes}, so the move
reads as a displacement rather than a mapping between two spaces --- and the one
that makes the dominant fact visible: of the $230$ rules that move, $96\%$ move
\textbf{straight down} --- $a$ unchanged, $b$ collapsed --- and \emph{all} $230$
decrease $b$. Not one moves horizontally. Dissipation destroys block size and
leaves the count alone. Most compact of the five.}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\ifgraphic{../../figures/fig_journal_slope_obc0.pdf}{0.42\textwidth}
\caption{\textbf{B --- slopegraph.} Neither coordinate, but the product $ab$ that
the exclusion curve constrains. Sectors left, attractors right, one line per
rule. Discards the $a/b$ split and keeps only what the theorem is about, which
makes the theorem the picture: everything starts on or above $2$, the unitary
rules run exactly horizontal, and almost everything else drops below.}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\caption{\textbf{C --- twin panels, horizontal.} The literal reading: sectors
left, attractors right, arrows across the gap. Easiest to caption and the least
ambiguous about which plane is which, but the arrows bundle in the gutter and
the gap costs about a third of the width.}
\end{figure}

\section*{Recommendation}

\noindent \textbf{A (overlay)} for a journal figure, with \textbf{B
(slopegraph)} as a panel beside it if space allows.

A is the most compact, and it is the only one that earns its compactness with a
finding: putting both coordinates on one set of axes shows that the arrows are
overwhelmingly \emph{vertical}. That is not visible in C, D or E, where the two
planes are drawn separately and the eye cannot compare positions directly. B then
adds the one thing A cannot show cleanly, which is where each rule sits relative
to $ab=2$ before and after.

C and D are the safest if the audience has not seen the two planes before, since
nothing has to be explained about hollow-versus-filled markers. E is the right
choice only if the asymmetry between the two maps is itself the point of the
section.
